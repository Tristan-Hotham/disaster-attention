    When a disaster takes lives, the place where it happened often becomes a name the whole country knows. I wanted to know how much the House of Commons talks about such a place afterwards, how long that lasts, and whether it depends on how many people died or on where the disaster happened. I counted, month by month, the Commons speeches that named the place of {{desc.n|int}} disasters in the UK between 1910 and 2020, in which {{desc.deaths_total|int}} people died.

    01 · The rise and the fadeNoticed at once, then less and less

    In the month of a disaster, Commons speeches naming the place rose to {{prose.m0|f1}} times their usual level (95% interval {{prose.m0_lo|f1}} to {{prose.m0_hi|f1}}), and to {{prose.m1|f1}} times the month after. By the fourth month the rise was about {{prose.m3|f1}} times. From the second year to the fifth it sat at about {{prose.late_min|f1}} to {{prose.late_max|f1}} times the usual level, and the intervals all include no change. In the two years before, the counts were flat, so the rise is not a trend that was already under way.

    For each disaster I also asked how long it took to reach half of all the extra attention over five years. The median was {{half.median|f1}} months; for {{half.within12|int}} of the {{half.n|int}} disasters with any extra attention it was within the first year. A few cases kept Parliament's attention much longer, usually because an inquiry, a trial or a campaign by families followed: Grenfell Tower reached half only after {{cases.grenfell.half_k|int}} months, Piper Alpha after {{cases.piperalpha.half_k|int}}, and Gresford after {{cases.gresford.half_k|int}}.

    02 · The death tollMore deaths, only a little more talk

    Across all the disasters, the raw link between deaths and attention is close to nothing (rank correlation {{desc.spearman_deaths_C|f2}}). Some disasters with many deaths left almost no trace in the searchable record: the {{cases.senghenydd.deaths|int}} deaths at Senghenydd in {{cases.senghenydd.year}} and the {{cases.quintinshill.deaths|int}} at Quintinshill in {{cases.quintinshill.year}} produce {{cases.senghenydd.C|int}} and {{cases.quintinshill.C|int}} matching contributions, partly because older volumes are harder to search and wartime reporting was restricted. Others with few deaths were discussed again and again: the derailment at Grayrigg in {{cases.grayrigg.year}}, in which one person died, came up in {{cases.grayrigg.C|int}} contributions.

    The analysis plan, written before I looked at the results, compares disasters with the same usual level of mentions, the same kind and era, and the same type of search word. In that model, twice the deaths goes with about {{prose.el_double_pc|f0}}% more attention over five years (95% interval {{prose.el_double_lo|f0}}% to {{prose.el_double_hi|f0}}%): more, but much less than in proportion. This link is not robust. Its interval includes zero in {{prose.n_checks_cross|int}} of the {{prose.n_checks|int}} checks I set out in advance, and shuffling the death tolls at random gives a link at least as strong in {{perm.p_two_pc|f0}}% of draws. A placebo test, which pretends each disaster happened three years earlier, finds nothing ({{prose.placebo_b|f2}}).

    Those are the first results, for {{desc.n|int}} disasters. Afterwards I found usable search words for {{ext.events.added_main|int}} more that I had left out, and added them under the same rules (the dark outlines on the map). With all {{ext.desc.n|int}}, twice the deaths goes with about {{ext.prose.el_double_pc|f0}}% more attention (95% interval {{ext.prose.el_double_lo|f0}}% to {{ext.prose.el_double_hi|f0}}%), and shuffling the tolls gives a link that strong in only {{ext.perm.p_two_pc|f1}}% of draws. The link is a little clearer, but still not robust: its interval includes zero in {{ext.prose.n_checks_cross|int}} of the {{ext.prose.n_checks|int}} checks. Because I added these disasters after seeing the first results, I show both versions.

    03 · Whose disasterLondon's lead rests on two disasters

    With the same deaths, kind and era, disasters in London and the South East drew {{prose.london_x|f1}} times the attention of those elsewhere in England (95% interval {{M1g.London and South East.lo|f0}}% to {{M1g.London and South East.hi|f0}}% more). But that gap rests on two London disasters: leave out Grenfell Tower and the King's Cross fire together, or use the other search word where one exists, and the difference shrinks to about {{prose.london_drop2_b|f0}}% with an interval that includes no difference. Disasters in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland were not clearly treated differently from those in England, and mining disasters were not clearly different from other kinds. The data are too thin to say more than that.

    04 · What is rememberedA list is already a memory

    Any list of disasters is a filter of what has been remembered. The Wikipedia list keeps disasters with 40 or more deaths; smaller ones are here only if Wikidata or the list of search words I started from included them, and those were chosen because people still talk about them. That shows: the {{data.events.src_harvest|int}} smaller disasters that came from the search list had a median of {{prose.harvest_median_deaths|f0}} deaths but were often much discussed, which flattens the link with the death toll. Among the {{prose.listonly.n|int}} disasters on the Wikipedia list alone, the link is much steeper (elasticity {{prose.listonly.b|f1}}), an exploratory result I did not plan.

    The search word matters too. People say "the Lockerbie bombing" more than "in Lockerbie": the phrase "in Lockerbie" appears {{cases.lockerbie.C|int}} times in the five years after 1988, the word "Lockerbie" {{swapcases.lockerbie.C|int}} times. Some words also mean other things. Hillsborough is a castle in County Down and the name of a 1985 agreement, so I left it out of the main analysis; "King's Cross" was also in debates about the Channel Tunnel railway in the same years, which I noticed only after the results were in.

    05 · How I did thisCounting speeches, month by month

    The disasters. I started from the Wikipedia list of disasters in Great Britain and Ireland by death toll, Wikidata's disasters in the UK, and a list of place words for well-known disasters. I kept single, local events on UK soil since 1909 with at least one death, and left out pandemics, heatwaves, smogs, losses at sea and events abroad. At first {{data.events.excluded|int}} candidates could not be used, most because no search word was available. After a second search for words, {{ext.events.excluded|int}} remain, for example where the only word is too broad ("Gatwick", "London Bridge", "7 July"); they are rings on the map.

    The counts. For each disaster I counted Commons speeches each month that contained "in" plus the town's name, or the name of the pit, station or village itself, from two years before to five years after, using the UK Parliament's Hansard search. The usual level is the average of the two years before, adjusted for how much Parliament was sitting and for the growth of Hansard.

    The models. The plan was written down before any model was run: a Poisson regression of five-year attention on the death toll, the usual level, the kind of disaster, the era and the type of search word, then {{prose.n_checks|int}} planned checks and a placebo. Exploratory checks are labelled as such.

    What this cannot say. It counts words, not care or action. A short list of speeches can matter more than a long one, and much of the response to a disaster happens outside the chamber, in inquiries, courts and communities. It is a comparison, not an experiment.
